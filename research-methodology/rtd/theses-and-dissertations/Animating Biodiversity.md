## **Abstract** 

The protection and preservation of biodiversity, a term that encompasses all living organisms on Earth, is becoming an exceedingly important topic as we are losing species at an alarming speed. Inspired by the UN conference, COP15, on biodiversity, this project seeks to engage and educate audiences about the importance of biodiversity conservation in the context of gardening, in close cooperation with two local gardening-related organisations based in Norrköping, Sweden. 

The design project utilises motion graphics as a medium to depict the interconnections between biodiversity and gardening practices visually. By combining animation, visual effects, and audio elements, the motion graphics attempt to communicate the significance of preserving biodiversity and sustaining healthy garden environments. This thesis essay explores the research question using a Research Through Design (RtD) approach. The project was conducted through research, concept- and prototype development, finalising in an evaluation and validation phase. 

The conclusion of this thesis suggests that motion graphics allow for a visual representation of complex environmental concepts, making them further accessible to a wide audience. By bridging the gap between scientific information and public understanding, motion graphics can empower individuals to adopt sustainable gardening practices and contribute to the preservation and protection of biodiversity. 

**Keywords:** Design, Research Through Design, Motion Graphics, Biodiversity, Gardening

## **1 Introduction** 

Biodiversity, a term which encompasses all living organisms on planet Earth, is becoming an increasingly urgent topic across governments, boards, companies and people all across the world. We are losing species at an alarming speed, and scientists claim we are facing a new mass extinction. According to the WWF's Living Planet Report 2022, data shows that in the last 50 years, the population of wild vertebrates (mammals, birds, amphibians, reptiles and fish) has decreased by 69 per cent. A report from the Intergovernmental Platform on Biodiversity and Ecosystem Services (IPBES) titled Global Assessment on Biodiversity and Ecosystem Services 2019 suggests that up towards one million species risk disappearing (WWF). This is the highest number in human history (WWF, n.d.). 

We need to act before we risk doing irreversible harm to the planet, which we hope will sustain us for many more generations to come. 

### **1.1 UN Biodiversity Conference (COP15)** 

In December of 2022, our world leaders met in Montreal, Canada at the UN Biodiversity Conference (COP15) for the UN convention regarding the topic of biodiversity. The purpose of COP15 was to agree on a global framework, which would help promote and protect biodiversity. The ultimate goal of the framework would be to halt and reverse nature loss by the year 2030. We desperately need to change our ways, or we risk damaging the biodiversity on planet Earth and our quality of life. Preserving nature is a critical aspect of meeting the Sustainable Development Goals as we aim to limit global warming to 1.5 degrees. Adopting a global biodiversity framework, as presented at COP15, can ensure that we not only prevent nature loss but also secure the health and well-being of our planet and future generations (UN, n.d.). 

**“Nature and biodiversity is dying the death of a billion cuts. And humanity is paying the price for betraying its closest friend. In the words of the UN Secretary-General, ʻwe are committing suicide by proxyʼ. This Conference of the Parties must secure the future of our planetary life support system.”** _– Inger Andersen, UN Under-Secretary-General and UNEP Executive Director_ 

The progression of COP15 ultimately led to a framework and suggested resources needed for its implementation. Among other topics, the conference addressed the threats of overexploitation, fragmentation, pollution and unsustainable agricultural practices. The outcome of participating nations was an agreement on adopting four overarching global goals by 2030. In sum, nations vow to protect 30% of Earthʼs lands, oceans, coastal areas and inland waters and to cut food waste in half by the year 2030 (CBD, 19 December 2022).

### **2.1 Biodiversity** 

First of all, it is relevant to understand the term ʻbiodiversityʼ, seeing as my ambition is to increase awareness about this topic. Biodiversity is a contraction of “biological diversity”, and refers to the variety of living organisms found in a given area, such as animals, plants, and microorganisms, as well as the genetic diversity which resides within each species. It encompasses a multitude of ecosystems, species interactions, and ecological processes which all contribute to the functioning and resilience of the planet (CBD, 1992). Biodiversity is essential to sustain life on Earth and provides a range of ecosystem services that are vital for human well-being and the health of the planet (Díaz et al., 2019; Millennium Ecosystem Assessment, 2005). It supports functions such as soil formation, pollination, pest regulation, and climate regulation, which in turn contributes to air quality, clean water, food security, climate stability, and cultural values (Cardinale et al., 2012; Hooper et al., 2005). 

Additionally, biodiversity holds an intrinsic value, representing the extraordinary diversity of life forms which has evolved over billions of years, each with unique characteristics and contributions to the web of life (Wilson, 1992). Given its fundamental importance, it is crucial to understand and conserve, seeing as it is sustaining human societies and natural systems upon which we depend. Let us further our understanding of biodiversity and the related concepts of ecosystems and food chains. 

#### **2.1.1 What is Biodiversity?** 

Biodiversity is said to encompass all living species, such as animals and plants, as well as fungi and bacteria (American Museum of Natural History, n.d.). The term does not only include species we consider rare, but all living things, from humans to tiny organisms, such as microbes and invertebrates. The range of life and species on the planet is vast, with many species yet to be discovered. Scientists estimate that there are approximately 8.7 million species on the planet. However, only 1.2 million species have so far been identified and the vast majority of that number being insects. The additional millions of undiscovered species are organisms that so far remain complete mysteries to us humans (National Geographic, n.d.). 

9 

All organisms and species alive on planet Earth today have evolved unique traits which separate them from one another. Scientists are interested in finding how much biodiversity there is on a global scale, seeing the number of species that are yet to be discovered. Another topic of interest is to study how individual species coexist within specific habitats. When referring to biodiversity it is certainly possible to look at the immense range of species on planet Earth. However, it can also be used to refer to a certain combination of species within specific ecosystems (National Geographic, n.d.). 

#### **2.1.2 Biodiversity: Ecosystems and Foodchains** 

An ecosystem can be described as a biological community within a geographical area where living species, such as plants, animals and other organisms, interact with each other, the landscape and the weather (National Geographic, n.d.). Ecosystems can be categorised into abiotic and biotic constituents, meaning nonliving and living parts. Abiotic parts of an ecosystem can be humidity, temperature, climate, soil, water, sunlight, minerals and all other non-living elements (National Geographic, n.d; Britannica, n.d.). Biotic parts of an ecosystem consist of all its living members, such as animals, plants and other living organisms (ibid.). The constituents are linked together by two major forces: the cycling of nutrients within the ecosystem and the flow of energy through the ecosystem. Ecosystems can be of varying size, while some are small enough to be composed within a single water droplet, others can be large enough to comprise entire landscapes and regions (Britannica, n.d.). 

The movement of organic matter and energy from a producer level through varying consumer levels composes a food chain. Many food chains within an ecosystemʼs biological community inter-lap, which forms what ecologists refer to as a food web. The final links of all food webs are made up of decomposers. These heterotrophs consist of mammals, insects, fungi and bacteria that break down dead organisms and organic waste into smaller components, later to be used as nutrients by producers. Nutrients are compounds and chemical elements that organisms must obtain from their surroundings to grow and sustain life. Autotrophs primarily obtain nutrients from the soil, while heterotrophs obtain nutrients primarily from other organisms (Britannica, n.d.). 

All factors within an ecosystem depend on the other factors either directly or indirectly. A change in an abiotic factor, such as temperature, can affect a certain plant which usually grows within an ecosystem and cause them to disappear. This can influence another link in the food chain, say an animal who would consume this plant. The animal would then have to adapt to this change, relocate to another ecosystem or succumb to its circumstances (National Geographic, n.d.).

### **2.2 Threats to Biodiversity** 

Approximately three-quarters of land-based environments and an estimation of 66% of ocean environments have been substantially altered. More than a third of the planetʼs land surface and nearly 75% of all freshwater resources have been claimed for crop or livestock production. Other human actions such as overfished oceans, polluted water sources, reduced forests and the creation of the current climate crisis are currently impacting biodiversity all around the world – from the most remote terrains to our very own backyard. Climate change impacts the stressors put on our nature and our well-being to a greater extent (WWF, n.d.). 

Russel (2022) identifies six of the greatest threats to biodiversity: habitat loss and degradation, invasive species, pollution, climate change, overexploitation of natural resources and human activity and population. Habitat loss and degradation happen as an area that initially was used as habitat is no longer inhabited by nature. Human actions such as deforestation, agriculture, mining and other industrial activities are o�en to blame as crucial habitat spaces for wildlife and plants are removed. However, natural events can also cause habitat degradation such as forest fires and hurricanes. Loss of biodiversity can also occur as invasive species are introduced within certain ecosystems. An invasive species is an organism, for example, a plant or an animal, that is non-native or unnatural to an ecosystem. These species threaten the plants and animals already residing within that ecosystem as they may outcompete them for space and nourishment. 

Pollution, as Russel (2022) explains, can occur in the water, air or land, and any form can be a threat to biodiversity. When toxic chemicals or products have entered ecosystems in the past, it has led to the extinction of certain species. Natural substances can also provide pollution if they are disproportionate within a specific area. Climate change is another threat to biodiversity. The increasing global temperatures have significant effects on environments, as seasons come earlier or later than expected, and can lead to some animals struggling to find resources to survive. Climate change has also led to a rise in sea levels and a decreasing amount of sea ice, thus affecting both animals in the sea and on land. It is believed that as climates and temperatures continue to change, the threat to biodiversity will only increase in severity. 

Overexploitation of natural resources is an additional threat to biodiversity and happens in correlation to certain human activities such as hunting, fishing and gathering of plants, animals and other natural resources. The loss of large populations of animals and/or plants in a short timespan can affect other wildlife within the same region. This can be illustrated with an example: if a primary predator is overfished within a sea habitat, there is nothing to keep populations of prey in check. As they become redundant, the food chain becomes unstable and upsets the once-established balance within the ecosystem. Human activity and 

11 

population is the sixth factor which is argued to have a significant impact on biodiversity (Russel, 2022). There are about 7 billion people on Earth now and by the year 2050, the population is expected to be at 10 billion people. About 200 years ago, less than 1 billion people resided on the planet. As the population continues to increase, the need for resources such as water, food and shelter will increase, while more human activity will occur. Biodiversity is believed to decrease because of these needs, as well as development and urbanisation. Humans will require space to live, work and play, so regions that were once filled with nature and diversity will become concrete jungles (ibid.). 

#### **2.2.1 Threats in the Garden** 

According to the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA), loss among species of plants has been identified to happen in direct correlation to human causes (n.d.). Some of the more common effects are loss of habitats, pollinators, dispersing agents, or other plant partners. Additionally, humans have introduced competing species and pests, usually not native to the area. This means that other species and pests have not co-evolved with certain plants, which then risks consequences to the established native plants. 

Within the context of gardening, the use of pesticides can have detrimental effects on biodiversity. Pesticides are used to target and eliminate pests, such as fungi, insects or weeds, but they can also harm beneficial insects, birds and other wildlife which contribute to a healthy and balanced ecosystem. Research has shown that the use of pesticides can lead to the decline of non-targeted species, disrupt food chains, and hurt overall biodiversity (Kovácz-Hostyánszki et al., 2017; Woodcock et al., 2016). Insecticides can, for example, affect pollinators such as bees and butterflies, leading to a decrease in pollination services and a potential decline in certain plant populations (Goulson, 2013; Potts et al., 2010). To protect and preserve biodiversity in gardening, it is suggested to adopt alternative approaches to pesticides to minimise harm to non-targeted organisms. 

### **2.3 Actions to Preserve Biodiversity** 

Preserving biodiversity requires joint efforts and proactive measures to both protect and restore ecosystems. One action is establishing and managing protected areas, such as national parks, reserves, and wildlife sanctuaries, which serve as a refuge for diverse species and ecosystems (CBD, 2011; Dudley et al., 2018). Strategies for conservation are also deemed necessary and should focus on habitat restoration and enhancement, such as reforestation, wetland rehabilitation, and the creation of green corridors which can facilitate the movement of species across different landscapes (Cardinale et al., 2012; Díaz et al., 2019). Additionally, sustainable land and resource management practices, such as organic farming and sustainable forestry, can minimise the negative impacts of human activities on biodiversity (Kremen & Merenlender, 2018). It is important to engage local communities and stakeholders in conservation efforts, promoting partnerships for 

12 

sustainable resource use and conservation practices. Public awareness and education also play significant roles in fostering a culture of biodiversity conservation, while promoting responsible consumption, and encouraging individual actions which contribute to the preservation of biodiversity (Gardner et al., 2017). 

#### **2.3.1 Urban Gardening and Agriculture** 

As cities and urban spaces have continued to expand, largely at the expense of natural and agricultural areas, the enthusiasm for urban agriculture has increased. Agriculture within an urban context can be complex to address, and the term urban agriculture (UA) is difficult to define accurately. This is as we find agriculture, an economic and spatial activity, o�en associated with rural landscapes. However, agriculture can be found both outside and inside cities (Royer et al., 2023). Betrand et al. (2005) describe UA as the “meeting between city and agriculture”. It seems that UA can be defined by its geographical location – within city areas. Still, it is difficult to delineate the distinction between rural, urban and peri-urban spaces as urbanisation is dynamic. The dynamics of agriculture, particularly within cities, follow the progress of needs and crises experienced by human societies (Royer et al., 2013). UA seems to be the result of certain societal needs, in terms of food but also social and cultural services (Lovell, 2010). An example of this would be the crisis in Cuba, where UA was used as a way to provide food security. Lovell and Johnston (2009) suggest that in countries of the Global North, UA is o�en a result of new social demand in urban areas, intending to provide an improved living environment by adding areas of recreation, cultural heritage and visual enhancements. 

Urban agriculture provides support to a wide range of ecological services, such as improved air and water quality, which benefit the nearby community (Consalès, 2003; Lovell and Johnston, 2009) as well as the biodiversity. Urban environments have long been neglected in initiatives aiming to conserve biodiversity (Abadie, 2008), they have in more recent years shown significant potential when it comes to hosting biodiversity (Saint-Laurent, 2000; McKinney, 2008; Di Pietro et al., 2018). However, the stress of human actions found within urban environments threatens urban biodiversity (Abadie, 2008). In such a context, UA could have the potential to aid urban biodiversity, as urban green spaces have the possibility of creating an ecological network within cities that is important for the preservation of biodiversity (Royer et al., 2023). 

#### **2.3.2 Gardening and Preserving Biodiversity** 

When looking to protect and preserve biodiversity within a gardening context, there are several actions individuals can take. Planting a diverse selection of native plant species can help create habitats and food sources for local wildlife while supporting pollinators and other beneficial insects (Tallamy, 2019). It is important to implement sustainable gardening practices, such as avoiding chemical pesticides to minimise harm to non-targeted organisms 

13 

and maintaining a healthy and balanced ecosystem (Goulson, 2013). One can install wildlife-friendly features such as birdhouses, bird feeders, bat boxes, and insect hotels, providing both shelter and nesting opportunities for a variety of species (Paker et al., 2014; Bradbury, 2019). Finally, designing and managing gardens which include diverse structural elements such as trees, shrubs, and water features can enhance habitat complexity and increase biodiversity (Kong et al., 2017; Lavorel et al., 2018). By using these actions within garden spaces, individuals can contribute to the conservation and protection of biodiversity. 

The concept of the “No Dig” method, also known as the “no-till” or “no-digging” approach, is a gardening technique which emphasises minimal disturbance to the soil structure. Instead of using traditional practices of digging or tilling the soil, this method advocates for the use of organic matter as a top layer or mulch on the soil surface (Frost, 2021). The primary objective of the “No Dig” method is to promote soil health and biodiversity by preserving the natural structure and composition of the soil (ibid.). By avoiding extensive digging, the method aims to protect organisms living in the soil, as well as minimise weed growth, and enhance water retention capabilities (Soil Association, n.d.). This method has gained popularity among gardeners and farmers as an environmentally friendly and sustainable approach to cultivation, which promotes healthier soil ecosystems and reduces the need for synthetic fertilisers and pesticides. 

### **2.4 Graphic Design and Visual Communication** 

Graphic design plays a significant role in shaping persuasive projects, as it combines both visual aesthetics with visual communication, to influence and engage audiences. By using various design elements such as colour, typography, layout, and imagery, graphic design has the power to create compelling visuals which enhance the effectiveness of persuasive messages (Landa, 2019). The persuasive potential of design lies within its ability to evoke emotions, convey information, and establish a visual hierarchy which guides the viewersʼ attention (Fogg, 2003). Whether it be through advertising campaigns, public service announcements, or social media graphics, the design enables the creation of visually impactful and persuasive content which can capture audiences and drive them towards desired actions. Through a thoughtful implementation of design principles and persuasive strategies, design can serve as a persuasive tool which can influence attitudes, behaviours, and decision-making processes. 

#### **2.4.1 The Role of Graphic Design** 

Barnard (2005) argues that the role of graphic design within communication can vary, and proposes four notions: informative, persuasive, decorative and magical. The first notion, informative, means that the role of graphics is to impart new knowledge. The information can be basic, such as simple signage in a public area, however, it can also be more sophisticated and used for things such as maps, diagrams and other illustrative works 

14 

providing information. The second role is persuasive, or rhetorical, meaning the job of the graphics is to persuade, convince or merely affect change in thought and/or behaviour. The third role, the decorative or aesthetic function, is when graphics are used in an entertaining or ornamental manner. Fourth, the magical function, which likely is the least obvious function, refers to two additional effects of graphic design. Barnard (2005) refers to Aumontʼs (1997) suggestion that symbolic images can grant ʻaccess to the sphere of the sacredʼ, which is exemplified by carrying a picture which encapsulates a certain memory. Secondly, via Tibor Kalman (1991), Barnard suggests graphic design being about ʻmaking something different from what it truly isʼ, which is described by the notion of having thirty graphic design students asked to produce something such as a garden will result in thirty different gardens, and therefore transforming one thing into another thing. 

#### **2.4.2 The Role of Senders and Receivers in Visual Communication** 

Within the context of visual communication, both senders and receivers play vital roles in the process of message transmission. The sender, o�en a designer or communicator, takes the responsibility of creating visual content to convey a specific message or idea (Shaughnessy, 2012). They implement various techniques to cra�visuals which can effectively communicate to and engage the intended audience. The senderʼs role includes understanding the target audience, tailoring the message to their needs, and employing persuasive strategies to elicit the desired responses (Arntson, 2016). On the other hand, the receiver of visual communication assumes the role of interpreting and understanding the message (Aielli & Parry, 2019). They actively engage in decoding symbols, signs, and additional visual cues embedded in the design, to derive meaning and make sense of the message. The interpretation from the receiver is influenced by their previous background and experiences, as well as cultural context and personal preferences. Effective visual communication occurs when the intended message from the sender aligns with the interpretation by the receiver (Williams & Newton, 2009). 

### **2.5 Environmental Communication** 

Effective environmental communication requires careful consideration of several important factors. Initially, it is crucial to understand the target audience, as you tailor the message to their knowledge, values, and attitudes towards the environment (Corbett & Durfee, 2004). Secondly, employing clear and accessible language is important to ensure the audienceʼs understanding (Sippel, et al. 2022). Avoiding scientific jargon and utilising relatable examples can make complex environmental issues further relatable and actionable. Additionally, implementing storytelling techniques can further facilitate empathy and emotional connections (Manney, 2008). Finally, utilising visual communication, such as imagery and infographics, can assist in conveying complex information and stimulate visual engagement (Sheppard, 2012). By considering these factors, environmental communication 

15 

can effectively convey messages and inspire behaviour change, while promoting sustainable attitudes and practices. 

#### **2.5.1 Challenges and Opportunities in Environmental Communication** 

Concepts such as biodiversity loss and climate change have been proven to be challenging to communicate in transdisciplinary research and/or organised community-focused activities (Goldman et al., 2018). On a local level, the topics can encounter a lack of comprehension, as representations of causes and effects can appear foreign to local perspectives. Research into transdisciplinary science communication (Jahn et al., 2012; OʼLear, 2015; Schönenberg et al., 2017) suggests that we are not only facing a communicative gap, but several tangible barriers when approaching this audience. One significant barrier is the complexity and the abstract nature of environmental issues, making it difficult for individuals to fully grasp and relate to the information (Moser, 2010). Another barrier can be limited access to information and educational resources, which risk hindering individuals from acquiring the necessary knowledge to engage in environmental discussions (Lorenzoni et al., 2007). Additionally, economic and time-related constraints can constitute barriers, as adopting environmentally friendly behaviours or purchasing sustainable products may be perceived as costly and/or time-consuming (Tanner & Kast, 2003). Furthermore, the lack of supportive infrastructure, such as public transportation systems or recycling systems, can also constitute a barrier and discourage people from taking environmentally responsible actions (Brody et al., 2008). To address these barriers, tailored communication is required which simplifies complex information, increases accessibility to educational information and resources, emphasises the affordability and ease of sustainable choices, and advocates for supportive infrastructure and policies. Thoughtful communication is deemed pivotal to successful research and project- and/or policy implementation (Dilling & Lemos, 2011; Leombruni, 2015; Ansari et al., 2021). 

The success of biodiversity goals depends on sufficient communication attributed to local communities. According to a study conducted on this very topic, the conclusions came to show that a critical understanding of local awareness, experiences with climate concerns and the local environment were key elements to communicating climate change (Ansari et al., 2021). Using a combination of different voices over different seasons was considered critical, as it reduced the bias in representing climate risk between different stakeholders and seasonal variability. Finally, the study suggests that being transparent with the research, being open-minded in informal exchanges with local community members and gathering an in-depth understanding also held great importance (2021).

### **3.1 Research Through Design** 

Research through Design (RtD), as introduced by Stappers and Giaccardi (2017), is a “designerly way of doing research”. This specific approach implies that the knowledge generation process is preceded and originated by various design actions. Therefore, the steps of investigating, iterating, and evaluating concepts constitute both research and result in the knowledge contribution (ibid.). The approach emphasises the creation of tangible artefacts and prototypes, as a means of inquiry, exploration, and knowledge production (Zimmerman et al., 2007). RtD consists of iterative cycles of design, implementation, evaluation and reflection, where designed artefacts can serve as probes to uncover new insights, discover new possibilities, and challenge existing assumptions (Frayling, 1993). 

This project will make use of this approach, iterating and reevaluating design decisions throughout the design process. I deem this to be a suitable approach, seeing as I want to discover how visual communication can be used to increase awareness about biodiversity. This project will attempt to constitute one possible route to do so, by using motion graphic design. The iterative cycles of design will allow me to reevaluate the choice of using motion graphics within this specified context, while hopefully ending in relevant and meaningful conclusions if this indeed can lead to increased awareness about biodiversity. 

### **3.2 Designing Motion Graphics** 

Motion graphics have emerged as an effective and popular medium for social media distribution, due to their inherent visual appeal, engaging storytelling capabilities, and compatibility with the dynamic nature of varying social media platforms. The fast-paced characteristics many social media platforms share today necessitate content which can capture viewersʼ attention quickly and effectively convey messages. Motion graphics, with animated visuals, typography, and transitions, can provide an eye-catching and immersive experience which can cut through the noise of social media feeds (Oentoro, 2022). Moreover, motion graphics can provide the advantage of being shareable, easily digestible, and suitable for several platforms and devices, making them well-adapted to the mobile-centric and on-the-go characteristics of social media consumption (Voramontri et al., 2019). By using motion graphics in social media distribution, content creators can 

17 

increase both reach and engagement and effectively communicate their message to the intended target audience. 

This motivated the choice of attempting to explore how motion graphics could be used to increase awareness about biodiversity among people with a gardening interest.

### **3.4 Interviews** 

Interviews can be used to acquire information regarding a particular problem, while also exploring personal attitudes, perceptions and opinions of the interviewees (Harrel & Bradley, 2009). Additionally, interviews can be used to achieve a greater understanding of the prior knowledge of individuals regarding a certain topic. According to Harrel and Bradley (2009), it is common to include both types of information. The method can vary in its structure and flexibility, meaning the interviewerʼs control over the respondentʼs answers 

19 

(ibid.). Three types of interviews are commonly distinguished for this purpose: unstructured, structured, and semi-structured (Harrel & Bradley, 2009). 

The format of an unstructured interview is characterised by having the interviewer ask questions with no particular direction. Therefore, the insights gathered are mostly dependent on what the respondent decides to share. It can lead to detailed and in-depth information, however, it is typically a lengthy type of data collection (Harrel & Bradley, 2009). Contrary to unstructured interviews, there are structured interviews. These are o�en characterised by having the interviewer ask fixed questions, which lack any type of flexibility. This type of interview is commonly used when the interviewer is looking for specific data which can be generalised and applied to a wider sample (ibid.). Finally, there are semi-structured interviews, which fall somewhere in the middle of the unstructured and structured interview formats. The interviewer makes use of a guide of structured questions, which are composed to collect data relevant to the research. However, these types of interviews are o�en flexible, as the interviewer can resort to improvised follow-up questions to achieve more in-depth information depending on the answers of the interviewee. According to Harrel and Bradley (2009), this type of interview format offers a more thorough understanding of the provided data. 

This research will make use of the semi-structured interview method, seeing as it allows for an opportunity to extract more in-depth information while also letting me look for certain types of data based on the questions I most hope to learn from respondents. I will use this method on two occasions in this process. The first is collecting initial insights, thoughts and opinions in the first step, “Preparation and Gathering of Information”, this study. The second time will be when evaluating and validating the prototypes in the last step, “Testing and Validation”, of this process. 

#### **3.4.1 Thematic Analysis** 

Thematic analysis is a widely used qualitative data analysis method, which involves identifying and analysing recurring patterns, themes and concepts with a certain dataset (Braun & Clarke, 2006). It provides an approach for organisation and interpretation of qualitative data, by capturing the underlying meaning of participantsʼ experiences and perspectives (Guest et al., 2012). The thematic analysis involves multiple iterative steps, including data familiarisation, coding, theme development, and refinement. The researchers immerse themselves into the data before engaging in the process of coding, where meaningful segments of data get assigned by codes or labels. These codes or labels are then sorted into broader themes, which capture commonalities as well as variations within the dataset. The thematic analysis allows the researcher to gain important insights into the complexities and nuances of participantsʼ experiences, resulting in a deeper understanding of the research phenomenon (Nowell et al., 2017). The approach is both 

20 

flexible and interpretative and can be applied across various disciplines which makes it a valuable tool within various qualitative research. 

In this project, thematic analysis will be used as a way to process and analyse the datasets collected from the semi-structured interviews. The analysis will allow for gaining important insights and further understanding of the participantsʼ experiences. 

### **3.5 Co-Design** 

Co-design, or in its extended term ʻcollaborative designʼ, can be defined as collective ingenuity and creativity throughout the design process (Steen et al., 2011). This method enables representative users to get involved in the process of creating, shaping and evaluating design concepts while providing valuable feedback. Exploring ideas together with the people representative of the intended target group ensures that the final artefact will achieve higher satisfaction from the users. Designers can increase their understanding of a situation by collaborating with future users through workshops, where various collaborating techniques such as brainstorming, clustering, and many others can be applied. 

#### **3.5.1 Brainstorming** 

Brainstorming, from a co-design perspective, is a participatory and collaborative method which brings together diverse stakeholders, including designers, users, and other relevant parties, to generate innovative ideas and solutions (Sanders & Stappers, 2012). Co-design emphasises the active involvement of all participants in a creative process, which aims to ensure that the resulting solution will meet both needs and aspirations of end-users (Sander, 2006). When conducting co-design brainstorming sessions, participants engage in a facilitated and inclusive environment, where everyoneʼs viewpoints and expertise are valued and respected. The ideas are generated through a combination of individual reflection and group discussions, allowing for an integration of multiple perspectives. The co-design approach to brainstorming facilitates collaborative problem-solving while encouraging empathy and promoting the exploration of new possible solutions through the interaction between the different participantsʼ knowledge and experience. 

#### **3.5.2 Clustering** 

Clustering is a technique o�en employed as a subsequent step a�er a brainstorming session, to organise and make sense of the generated ideas (Hannafin et al., 1999). The process involves grouping related ideas based on their thematic similarities, which creates clusters or categories representing specific themes or concepts. Clustering allows for the identification of patterns and connections while facilitating the exploration of common threads and potential openings for further exploration. The technique can be especially helpful when condensing and streamlining a large quantity of generated ideas into more 

21 

manageable clusters, which enables a more focused and structured analysis of the ideas. Additionally, clustering invites and encourages participants to engage in the sense-making process, while promoting a deeper understanding of collective ideas and identifying areas for further exploration and refinement.

## **8 Discussion and Conclusion** 

This chapter initially leads a discussion based on the research question for this thesis, regarding the theoretical framework and gathered insights. Therea�er, this chapter intends to summarise the main conclusion of this project. Additionally, it presents some suggestions for future work. 

### **8.1 Discussion** 

This project was inspired by the proceedings of COP15, the UN:s conference about biodiversity in late 2022, and how conversations about the preservation and protection of biodiversity are becoming exceedingly important. The topic of biodiversity is broad, seeing as it encapsulates all living organisms on the planet, from the largest species down to the variation of genetic material residing in each cell. There is truly much to learn and unpack within the topic, but I could not make this design project too extensive as it would not fit within the time constraints of this thesis work. I framed this topic, focusing on the gardening practices concerning biodiversity. 

The focus of this project was to explore how visual communication can be used to increase awareness of biodiversity. This process explores one potential route to do so, focusing on biodiversity among individuals aged 40 and up with a gardening interest. I wanted to attempt to reach this group, seeing as biodiversity is a crucial part of gardening, seeing as it supports the health and resilience of ecosystems and contributes to the overall success of garden practices. My preparatory interviews found that the preexisting knowledge and awareness regarding biodiversity varied greatly, and I hoped that the production of design artefacts could result in one possible way for increasing awareness. 

The results of this study lead me to believe that motion graphics could be a potential strategy to increase awareness. Motion graphics can be a powerful tool in environmental communication, offering unique opportunities to engage and educate audiences on otherwise complex environmental issues (Sheppard, 2012). Combining animation, visual effects, audio elements, and motion graphics allows for effective visualisation and storytelling of environmental concepts (Manney, 2008). Motion graphics can simplify complex information, make it more accessible and further engage viewers. I have found that this way of communicating has been met with a lot of enthusiasm, both from my collaborative sendersʼ and the potential receiversʼ I have met with. Motion graphics already have a lot of visual appeal and engaging storytelling capabilities in their favour, which can cut through the noise of social media feeds (Oentoro, 2022). I think motion graphics could have the potential of being an effective tool for communicating about environmental topics, such as biodiversity. Biodiversity is a complex concept which involves a wide range of species, ecosystems, and interactions, suggesting it could be more difficult to communicate 

55 

with traditional static visuals alone. Motion graphics offer the opportunity to visually represent interconnections and dynamics of biodiversity in a captivating and accessible way. Within the context of environmental communication, one of the significant barriers that have been identified is the complexity and abstract nature of environmental issues, which potentially makes it difficult for individuals to grasp and relate to the information (Moser, 2010). Other identifiable barriers have been limited access to information and educational resources, economic and time-related constraints, and lastly a supportive infrastructure (Lorenzoni et al., 2007; Bordy et al., 2008). This suggests that presenting such information via motion graphics could be motivated, however, I would think this should be motivated further by examining how these barriers could be approached within the validation phase. I believe that from this project alone it would be difficult to come to any direct conclusions on how this method would work when being implemented within a real scenario, seeing as I only had the opportunity to evaluate the prototypes to a very limited number of people. 

Throughout this process I have worked with both senders and receivers of information, making use of a project collaboration with the organisations Stadsodling Norrköping and Norrköping Trädgårdssällskap. Meanwhile, I have conducted interviews with representative individuals from the intended audience and target group. The organisations Stadsodling Norrköping and Norrköping Trädgårdssällskap acted within the role of senders, aiding the co-creation efforts of designing and communicating towards a target group they were much familiar with (Shaughnessy, 2012).My role as a designer did also fall within these frames, seeing as my goal was to create visual content deemed compelling and informative material which could effectively convey the importance of biodiversity in gardening practices. The project collaboration helped to ensure that the information and visual expression were accurate when trying to do so. 

On the other hand, the receiversʼ role has been to actively engage with the visual materials, interpret the message, and make connections between biodiversity and gardening practices (Aielli & Parry, 2019). This is where I made use of asking and evaluating the project and its process with respondents representative of the target group. I believe that understanding the perspectives, interests, and knowledge levels of the target audience, while working in close collaboration with stakeholders that hold much experience and knowledge, can help designers to create visually compelling and accessible materials which can communicate the benefits of biodiversity in gardening. Involving stakeholders such as Stadsodling Norrköping and Norrköpings Trädgårdssällskap throughout this process has been great when working with the communication of this type of topic, as it has allowed me to get rich insight without having to rely on desk research or quantitative data selection methods such as surveys. This collaboration suggests that the artefacts may achieve a higher satisfaction from the users, however, this is difficult to prove. 

56 

The collaboration aspect of this project has also been interesting within the context of environmental communication, where some theories suggest that local awareness and climate concerns within the local environment were considered key elements to understanding climate change (Ansari et al., 2012). This suggests that maybe the same logic could be applied to a topic such as biodiversity. I tried to include local contextual cues within my prototypes, which went unnoticed during the evaluation phase. However, I could potentially see that the environments that were visualised, such as the park and the garden, could still feel familiar to the garden-interested individuals within my target group. 

Overall, I would have liked more time to test out revisions based on the feedback from the evaluation phase. However, even then I think the small number of semi-structured interviews does not supply enough basis to confidently land conclusions on how well this approach works for the intended target group. The broad inlet of the topic, leading into both discussions of backyard gardening as well as urban gardening could probably have been framed more strictly to get more applicable results for one more specific scenario. It has been challenging to describe the complexity of biodiversity, the relationship between gardening and biodiversity, and present specific actions, within the time-related restrictions given on each prototype. Ideally, I would have liked more time to evaluate the scripts and storyboards further with both sendersʼ and receiversʼ and gather more input on the information I wished to capture with my motion graphics. I can see that the result could have been much improved with more time, more input from stakeholders, and further discussions on distribution channels. 

#### **8.1.1 Future Work** 

The initial idea for this project was that the prototypes developed in collaboration with the local gardening organisations could be used on social media platforms for respective organisations, or even a joint website where the videos could be presented. This website could contain information about both organisations and hopefully give them an additional opportunity to reach new potential members. I would have liked to put more time and effort into cra�ing these prototypes with a strategy in mind, further exploring the best possible distribution channel to use. I think that if I would have had the time to do so, I could have come to more developed conclusions about how the format and approach of communicating this information via motion graphics is a good strategy to reach and increase awareness among my intended target group. In sum, future work of this project could further focus on the response of motion graphics concerning biodiversity awareness among social media platforms and also look into understanding the target groupsʼ role within different social media platforms.

### **8.2 Conclusion** 

The purpose of this thesis has been to explore how visual communication can be used to increase awareness regarding biodiversity. This project proposes one possible route to do so, by employing motion graphic design. 

The dynamic and immersive nature of motion graphics allows for a visual representation of complex environmental concepts, making them further accessible and engaging to a wide audience. By showcasing interconnections and the importance of biodiversity in garden-related practices, motion graphics can be used to convey the need for conservation and sustainable gardening approaches. Through animation, visual effects, and audio elements, motion graphics can be used to capture viewersʼ attention, evoke emotional responses, and inspire action towards biodiversity conservation. By bridging the gap between scientific information, such as complex environmental communication, and public understanding, motion graphics hold the potential to empower individuals to adopt sustainable gardening practices and contribute to the preservation of biodiversity. 

As a versatile medium, motion graphics can continue to play a vital role in raising awareness, fostering appreciation, and further driving positive change for the protection of biodiversity in the future.
