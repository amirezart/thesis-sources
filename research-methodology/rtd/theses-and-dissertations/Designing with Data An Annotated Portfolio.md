This paper presents an account of the Interaction Research Studio's distinctive approach to incorporating data into design, unpacking a vital but previously unexamined facet of our two-decade-long research programme. We demonstrate how data has served as a pragmatic tool in creating engaging, contextually-aware computational products that provide alternatives to dominant task-oriented and consumerist narratives by promoting interpretation, imagination, spirituality, and socio-cultural reflection. Leveraging an annotated portfolio approach, we revisit our diverse portfolio of designs, starting from individual reflections before surfacing overarching themes. This paper foregrounds our strategies for utilising data, the associated labour, and the challenges that have arisen throughout our design process. This emergent and empirical account may complement more theoretical treatments of designing with data and provide valuable insights for researchers, designers, and practitioners navigating the complexities of data-integrated design in the contemporary technological landscape.

#### **1 INTRODUCTION** 

For the last twenty years and more, we have been engaged in a research programme exploring alternatives to the visions offered by dominant (and dominating) technology companies. Our designs explore different activities, forms, styles of interaction, values and most of all, different relationships with technology than the largely task-oriented, instrumental, and consumerist products available for purchase. We investigate alternative possibilities through a form of research-throughdesign in which we conceive, make and deploy novel computational research products [7,47] in a variety of settings ranging from homes to monasteries to gardens, and study them during field trials that often last months or years. Ours is a highly 

empirical practice, then, in which we value the emergent nature of design practice and the polyphonic, interpretative nature of empirical encounters over abstract theory either as a guide or outcome of design research [19,20,29]. 

From the beginning, many of our designs have incorporated data as a design material in one way or another. For the most part, this is not because data, or designing with it, has been at the centre of our research interests. Instead, it has typically been a pragmatic response to issues of how to make devices that are engaging over time and that encourage people to orient themselves conceptually and behaviourally to the situations around them. Moreover, though there are clear family resemblances amongst many of our designs, we have not explicitly aimed to apply techniques from one design to others, or to draw more general lessons about using data. On the whole, designing with data for us has been an often-unremarked means to achieve design goals such as encouraging imagination and exploration, supporting spirituality, or reflecting on sociocultural differences and inequality. 

Over recent years, however, investigations of data, often in conjunction with machine learning, have become increasingly widespread in interaction design and HCI. Work includes explorations of the nature of data and people’s experiences with it [14,15,17,44,48], an increasing number of devices and systems that use data in various ways [1,36,38,40,43,45,46,49,53] and more general considerations of data as a design material [3,42]. This burgeoning line of research has prompted us to reflect on our own designs, and to articulate in a more focused way how we have been using data as a material in our designs over a long period, with earliest examples dating back twenty years at the time of writing. Furthermore, the focus of this special issue on uncertainty prompted us to reexamine our longstanding themes of ambiguity and interpretation [32,51] in respect to designing with data. 

In this paper, then, we step back and provide an introspective account of how we have used data in our designs, the approaches we’ve explored, techniques we’ve developed, and challenges we’ve discovered. We do this from the bottom up, using the annotated portfolios approach we have discussed elsewhere [9,18]. This involves starting with descriptions of and reflections on the individual designs we’ve produced, before discussing themes that pertain to subsets of our designs. Our themes start with strategies for using data in design, then turn to the work involved in using data, and end with a set of challenges and issues in designing with data. In keeping with our design-led research methodology, this is a form of abstraction that is emergent in the sense that it builds from the things we have designed rather than any a priori theoretical concerns or research questions. It is also empirical in that, while we do abstract approaches, methods and issues, we maintain close connection between our abstractions and the things (our designs) that we abstract from – and we indicate this by including images of our designs regularly throughout our account, to remind us and our readers of what it is exactly that we are abstracting across.

Local Barometers (2008) are small devices designed to be encountered at different places around the home – nestled between books on a shelf, hanging from a towel rack, or plugged into a wall socket, for instance [25]. **Datacatcher (2016)** is a handheld device with a screen on one end and an inset rotary controller [6,8,24]. #### **1.2 Annotating a Portfolio** 

In the next section, we annotate these designs as a portfolio to highlight some of the strategies they embody for designing with data. Annotated portfolios are an approach suggested by John Bowers and Bill Gaver [9,18] for capturing lessons from design-led research without over-abstraction. They argue that while design outputs may represent various multifaceted and heterogeneous insights, these are not necessarily self-evident. Instead, they must be distinguished and highlighted, almost certainly using language. The language used to articulate the important features of designs – for instance, the conceptual insights they suggest – loses definition and particularity, however, if it is divorced from the designed artefacts to which it originally referred. In LaTour’s [10] terms, “theories, now made abstract and autonomous objects, float like flying saucers” if they are untethered from design outputs, and potentially drift into unproven or erroneous speculation. Thus, Bowers and Gaver suggest that maintaining an indexical relationship between annotations and (representations of) design outputs is a more satisfactory approach for capturing design lessons. Naming design features allows them to be more easily recognised in particular designs and hints at their potential generalisability. Retaining their connection to those designs, however, both particularises the named features and indicates that they might not apply similarly elsewhere. Greater potential generalisability may be achieved by annotating multiple designs formed into a portfolio by annotations: by indicating how a given conceptual insight applies to more than one design, its application in multiple situations can be appreciated, still without losing sight of the particularities of those situations. 

We follow this logic in the next section, in which we offer annotations of the ten designs described above, or subsets of them, to reflect on ways that we have used data to create them. Our method for developing these was fairly simple: we gathered images as proxies for the designs themselves and then grouped and arranged them as we discussed their commonalities and differences. The process of developing the annotated portfolios led to the final versions in a seamless manner, without any hidden or extraneous representations or rules, as we developed the annotations and groupings of designs in parallel. In this sense, annotating portfolios served simultaneously to support analysis and to represent its results. 

It is worth noting several things about this process. First, there was no stopping rule, and we could conceivably have developed more annotations. We finished when we judged that we had articulated the main features of our approaches to using data. Second, this portfolio of designs could be annotated in different ways, and indeed this has been done elsewhere [9]. The version we develop here focuses explicitly on how the designs deal with data as opposed to their interaction styles or the topics they address. Finally, annotated portfolios do not imply a particular form of presentation. Here we follow a style similar to those used by Gaver and Bowers [18,20] and others [37], however many others are possible.

#### **2.1 Framing Data** 

The first thing to note is that the designs described above all frame data to produce various effects (see Figure 11). 

Data are often assumed to be decontextualised collections of facts and information that are ideally agnostic about how they will be used to support new understandings or actions. For instance, information about local population numbers might be used by the government to plan schools, or by companies to devise marketing campaigns, or by campaigners to argue for or against growth. The data itself sits outside these uses, ready to be used for any of them – in Latour’s [10] terms, they are immutable mobiles, ready to be mobilised for various uses without themselves changing. 

These designs, in contrast, all seek to frame data in novel ways to make them salient, locally relevant and often to raise or highlight topics that might be of concern (c.f.[2]). History Tablecloth, for instance, frames data about the relative load on a table’s four legs as an indication of how long objects have been left upon it, to highlight the flow of objects in the home [12]. Plane Tracker frames data about the identities of passing aircraft into a visualisation of their flight paths, implicitly raising issues ranging from tourism to climate change. Prayer Companion frames data from news and social 

14 

media sites as being relevant for prayer. Though, as we will see, different strategies are used to frame data across the portfolio, all these designs share in common the intention highlight data as the ‘topic’ of the experience. 

It is worth noting that framing data not only joins these designs into a coherent group, it also differentiates the group from others, including those of our own (shown greyed-out in Figure 11) which do not frame data as their primary tactic for creating effects. For instance, My Naturewatch Camera [26] uses motion detection to trigger image capture, but even though motion detection is a form of data processing this is only incidental to My Naturewatch Camera’s primary purpose of affording wildlife photography. Similarly, Yo-Yo Machines [23] use capacitive touch switches to trigger messages shared by two remote devices, but the capacitive sensors are not used as data but merely control signals. The notion of framing data not only articulates a group of designs we think of as similar, it actively creates that group and separates it from a wider field of designs. 

#### **2.2 Uncertainty and Interpretation** 

The designs considered frame data as their topic, but they do it in a way that creates or maintains uncertainty and provokes interpretation [32,51]. Usually, the uncertainty is not so much about the veridicality of the data as it is about their interpretation and import. 

For instance, using weights to guide the flow of imagery in the Drift Table is straightforward, but without any narrative about what the piece is for or what tasks it might help people perform, the question it poses is about why people might use it and how they might incorporate it into the meaningful activities of their lives. Similarly, the History Tablecloth may highlight the length of time objects have been left upon it in a way that is unusual in everyday life, but this technical novelty is of less interest than questions of how people might interpret and act on this information once it is brought to the fore. The Indoor Weather Stations highlight air movement, light and temperature as a way of proposing that these things are important, without specifying how or why. In each case, we chose these data because our site research suggested that they might be of interest in the situations for which we were designing. The devices themselves, however, make no attempt to support users in deciding how to interpret the meaning of the data they highlight or their potential significance. 

Other designs use similar tactics to encourage people to develop interpretations about topics based on data that is potentially noisy or not directly suited for the task. For instance, the Local Barometer suggests that people might find clues about nearby narratives in information about what they seek to buy or sell. The Home Health Horoscope uses sensor data from sites that participants told us were significant, but these can only be indicative at best. Our use of horoscope fragments from the popular press is an attempt both to overinterpret the data as definite-sounding diagnoses/predictions and simultaneously to undermine the systems’ authority by invoking a genre that most readers only take semi-seriously. These systems use data to offer hints or clues about issues and topics while leaving open to participants’ interpretation what those issues and topics might be and how the data can be used to shed light on them. 

While designs such as the History Tablecloth, Indoor Weather Stations and Home Health Horoscope focus on data and topics that are local to the devices, others use data to evoke issues of wider societal relevance. For example, the Plane Tracker highlights local air traffic, and thus potentially (and in practice) suggests interpretation in terms of the desirability of travel, political and social conditions in the areas traversed, climate change and so forth. The Prayer Companion combines headlines with social media content to raise the issue of what global events might be worth bringing to the attention of God. The Energy Babble combines data and discussion about energy use to encourage thinking both about the topic itself and the ways it is addressed in the UK. The Datacatcher, finally, combines diverse streams of data as a goad and resource for orienting to sociocultural and economic issues, potentially from a political standpoint. 

15 

Thus, all the designs discussed here frame data as a way of raising topics of potential interest without resolving how they should be addressed.  They create uncertainty and encourage people to make their own sense despite it. Each design achieves this in idiosyncratic and often complex ways, however. This makes comparisons among them complex, and a complete analysis of the space they create all but impossible. Nonetheless, we discuss some broad trends in the strategies they use in the following sections. 

#### **2.3 Captured v Readymade Data** 

One simple distinction amongst our designs separates those that use captured data from those using readymade data. By “captured data” we mean data that we gather ourselves, usually using sensors, and represent in various ways (c.f. [1,16]). For instance, History Tablecloth uses data generated by four load sensors to create its effects. Temperature Tape, one of three Indoor Weather Stations, has two temperature sensors on either end of a long fabric tape (itself printed with thermochromic ink to indicate temperature gradients along its length) and a dial that shows the difference between the two readings to highlight temperature variations in the home. Home Health Horoscope amalgamates sensor data from half a dozen sensors to estimate the emotional state of a household. In each case, captured data is the ‘subject’ of the device, with a variety of means used to represent that data in ways that highlight its meaning for a particular situation. 

In contrast, many of our designs use readymade data to create their effects. By ‘readymade’, we refer to datasets that are created independently elsewhere, and for different purposes, to which we manage to get access (we use the term ‘readymade’ by analogy to Duchamp’s appropriation of readymade objects such as bicycle wheels and urinals as art [35]; see also [44]). For instance, Drift Table contains about a terabyte of high-definition aerial photography collected by GetMapping.com, Local Barometer uses local advertisements posted to a website, and Photostroller gives access to the vast archive of publicly accessible photography on the Flickr website.

#### **2.4 Types of readymade data** 

There are innumerable readymade datasets that can be used in design (figure 12). Some are relatively stable, or even fixed in the case of the aerial photography used in the Drift Table, which GetMapping.com gathered in 1999 for their “millennium map” of England and Wales (see [54]). Others, such as the RSS feeds used by the Prayer Companion or the Twitter streams sampled by the Energy Babble, are much more dynamic, with changes that are evident daily or even hourly. Most, of course, lie in between. For instance, new Flickr images are uploaded continuously, but these are added to a dataset so large that the additions are not easily noticeable. Similarly, Google Earth is regularly updated, but the changes are seldom noticeable and, in any case, the ‘ground truth’ (no pun intended) changes slowly. Stability, in other words, is a dimension more easily discerned at its extremes than in between. Nonetheless, it is a useful one to distinguish because stable and dynamic datasets can be used in different ways, as we will discuss later. 

17 

In addition, readymade datasets can be mixed to complicate and defamiliarise the data used in designs. For instance, Prayer Companion mixes RSS news headlines with quotes from social media to produce a dataset that suggests links amongst individual and global events, and thus plausibly reflects the current zeitgeist. Energy Babble mixes national grid statistics with personal and institutional tweets to create a stream of energy-related content that is sometimes all but incoherent, echoing inconsistencies in the national discourse. Datacatcher mixes data ranging from real estate prices and crime statistics to the number of pubs nearby, and even suggests comparisons among them, to give a sense of local sociocultural realities that goes beyond any single perspective. In each of these examples, mixing datasets creates synergies that, though somewhat unpredictable, can be useful in design. 

#### **2.5 Strategies for Using Readymade Data** 

From our perspective, using readymade data in interactive designs has the advantage that datasets are often vast, and thus we can devise relatively simple strategies for accessing the data that nonetheless produce near infinite results. Needless to say, creating desired effects with particular systems depends on the datasets chosen and often keyword or similar searches into those datasets. This is not enough, however, because the results are still likely to be overwhelming. A key factor in producing compelling systems, then, has to do with the strategies used to select which content to show at a given time. In this section, we describe some of the strategies we have used to suggest the rich possibilities of working with readymade datasets.

#### _2.5.1 Control+Drift_ 

Control+Drift can be a powerful strategy for creating compelling engaging experiences from readymade data (see [9]). Conceptually, the idea is to place the user in some conceptual area of a dataset – whether the ‘space’ is defined geographically or semantically – and then allow the system to wander over nearby data and present it without explicit control. 

For instance, the Photostroller was designed to give older people access to the vast Flickr database of photography. To provide some sort of control and order to the experience, we designed a handheld controller with a rotary dial allowing selection of one of seven pre-determined categories of image, and a slider allowing the searches to “detune” the selection 

18 

to include images that were more or less tangentially related to the search term. From that point, returned images were displayed as a kind of endless drifting slideshow, with no further interaction required. 

Similarly, Drift Table was typically programmed to start by showing the view from above its actual location (e.g., the home of a participant), before scrolling the images towards the centre of gravity of weights placed on the tables’ surface. Again, the experience was one of drifting from a starting point, allowing the device to show a continuously varied output even if users only interacted with it occasionally. (Note that although load sensors were used similarly to History Tablecloth, the focus was on accessing readymade imagery rather than highlighting the sensor data about weights: the Drift Table looks out at the world, while the History Tablecloth looks in at the home.) 

Control+Drift is particularly useful when the readymade data itself, and the way it is framed, is the point of the design. For instance, Photostroller was devised primarily to allow its older users to access Flickr images as a resource for reminiscence, discussion, or simply as an escape from the care home in which it was located. To a great degree, it didn’t matter much what the images were, as long as they were engaging for the users. Similarly, Drift Table was originally conceived to create a kind of opening in the home, to free it from its surroundings and connect it with the wider world (c.f [39]). We didn’t think it mattered much where it went — and when users surprised us by undertaking purposeful journeys, this emphasised the power of the ‘control’ part of the equation: in this case, the drift they created was not aimless, but a sustained trajectory towards a goal.

A second strategy involves using captured data to control a path through readymade data to establish meaningful links between their content. 

For instance, Home Health Horoscope uses data captured from local sensors to guide the construction of horoscopes from a large corpus of readymade data. The system was custom-made for participants, who informed the sensor designs with their stories of emotionally salient places and events in their homes – for instance, a cushion where the father would sit to chat with his wife over morning coffee. Sensor readings, for instance of the start time and duration of morning chats, were mapped to a set of emotional dimensions intended to capture the emotional wellbeing of the home. These, in turn, were used to select from a corpus of hundreds of sentences, each of which had been culled from horoscopes written for 

19 

popular consumption and represented along the same emotional dimensions. Mapping sensor data to the readymade horoscope dataset in this way allowed bespoke horoscopes to be generated daily as a way of indicating the potential import of what was sensed. 

Similarly, Local Barometer uses wind direction and speed, sensed just outside the house where it is located, to select online advertisements based on their origin upwind from the home. Selecting advertisements in this way transforms them into clues about the neighbourhoods surrounding the device’s location. Captured data about the wind is extended to indicate the nearby areas over which it has travelled, while a generic set of want ads become clues about who and what might be found in those areas. 

In each of these examples captured and readymade data are linked to be mutually illuminating. What will be illuminated and to what effect depends on the nature of the captured data collected, and the content of the readymade dataset. Like Control+Drift, this strategy for using readymade data is useful and can undoubtedly lead to many more rich experiences than we have explored here.

A third, and oft-used, strategy is to use location to select a subset of data from a larger dataset. This is the strategy behind commercial navigation systems, for example, which use GPS data to select which portions of an online map to display. 

Though location-based services are a well-trodden area, there is still scope to create innovative systems using this strategy. For instance, Datacatcher uses its location to reach into hundreds of governmental and commercial datastreams holding information about people – their demographics, income, buying behaviour and so on. Rather than using location simply to show where the device is or what shops and services might be, the effect created by the Datacatcher is to paint a rich picture of its sociocultural surroundings. When these are collected over the course of a journey, moreover, comparisons are invited, for instance about the effects of income inequality on health, or of the availability of public services on happiness. 

More complex and unexpected experiences can be created, moreover, by using other sources of location-dependent captured data. For instance, Plane Tracker uses captured data about the identity of passing aircraft to find and recreate their 

20 

flightpaths in a modified version of Google Earth. The views this produces implicitly provides information about local air traffic and compensates users for the noise of overhead planes. Simultaneously, the arrangement works to reframe the vast dataset of aerial photography offered by Google Earth to become locally relevant. In other words, displaying locally sensed aircraft in terms of their journeys augments what can be sensed locally (either by a human or aerial), and at the same time particularizes a generic world map to be about those journeys. 

Considered abstractly, using location to index readymade data simultaneously particularises the readymade data to be relevant to location, and extends the captured location with the readymade information. Clearly this is a productive strategy, given the number of location-aware applications available already. We hope the examples here indicate that there is still great potential in using this strategy to create alternatives to the ways it is typically used in commercial products.

A fourth strategy for using readymade data exploits dynamic data to create experiences that change continuously and are linked to contemporary events. 

For example, Prayer Companion shows a stream of messages framed to suggest its relevance to nuns’ prayers of intercession. Our aim was to reflect content at a range of scales from individual to global, and a key requirement was that it should be, in the nuns’ terms, “pertinent”. Conceptually, this was quite simple to achieve. On the one hand, we chose readymade datastreams – a combination of RSS news feeds from around the world, and social media posts collected by WeFeelFine.org – that ensured a mixture of concerns from worldwide to local, and then relied on their dynamic nature to ensure that the results remained up-to-date and topical. 

Similarly, Energy Babble was designed to mix energy-relevant content from a variety of readymade and locally-crafted sources to create a kind of mirror to the complex energy discourses its users encountered as they tried to promote sustainable practices locally. Rather than seeking to produce well-organised outputs, we purposively wanted to exaggerate the confusing and sometimes contradictory discourses around energy that our participants from various energy communities already faced. We achieve this by sampling data from a wide range of Twitter feeds, as well as websites linked to those feeds and their messages, governmental sites and so forth. The dynamic nature of these datasets meant that 

21 

once we had chosen them, we could rely on the passage of time to produce a requisite range and mixture of messages, and to ensure that these remained topical. 

This strategy, of sampling dynamic datasets over time to ensure variability and timeliness, is perhaps the simplest of the four we discuss here, but also it is arguably the most elegant (c.f. [46] for an alternative approach for using time to structure data). Rather than relying on sophisticated searches to produce relevant data, simply drawing from appropriate streams allows bespoke systems to inherit the topicality these readymade sources are designed to capture. 

#### **3 WORKING WITH READYMADE DATA** 

The strategies we outline above have proven to be effective in designing compelling systems with data, but the practical considerations associated with crafting this data to facilitate new experiences within these spaces are manifold. Viewed from one angle, all our research products essentially act as sophisticated data processing units, repurposing captured, input or readymade data to varying extents based on the specific design. This is a facet of our practice that, although not often discussed, is both resource-intensive and requires a meticulous attention to detail that matches, if not surpasses, the physical manifestation of our design work. 

Broadly, our work with data falls into three distinct categories: data wrangling, post-processing data and hybrid data, which we describe below. 

#### **3.1 Data wrangling** 

This process involves the extraction and parsing of readymade data, which can appear in many forms and must be untangled to render it useful. For instance, the data used in the Drift Table project encompassed a terabyte of JPG images. A bespoke software application [34] was required to assemble these images and arrange them into a geographically accurate map of the UK. The view onto this map was controlled by data from load cells that provided a direction of drift based on shifting surface weight. In addition, the Cartesian coordinates encoded into the JPGs could subsequently be used to identify and display the names of villages, towns, boroughs, and cities on a small text display situated on the side of the table. 

Some of the data used in the Datacatcher project was gathered from large repositories, publicly available from government sources like census records, crime statistics, or public health data. This kind of data is not updated frequently and so could be harvested once to be transformed, geocoded, and sorted, rendering it usable as a project resource. This required the construction of a project-specific data repository, which formed the foundation for a stream of geolocated messages. 

A notable advantage of both the Drift Table's imagery and some of the Datacatcher's large repositories was the static nature of the data. The data did not dynamically change, contrasting with other projects that required real-time processing of online data, contingent on stable internet connections to various resources. The Local Barometer, for instance, was developed before the widespread use of APIs. Consequently, we devised a system that would search a classified ads website by postcode and parse the webpage in real-time to extract the necessary data. This system proved efficient as long as the host didn't implement any unexpected website changes that could impede the parser's functionality. 

#### **3.2 Post-processing data** 

With only few exceptions the data used in all our devices has undergone transformative post-processing before it is used. For instance, the data for Datacatcher were not merely tagged with a geolocation, but also transformed into standalone sentences. These sentences were crafted and curated to foster a new experiential appreciation of the raw data, enabling it to be presented more like natural language reasoning. Examples include, “They say unemployment around here is running 

22 

at 25%, that sounds like a lot” or “Life expectancy here in Kensington is 15% higher than when you were in Tower Hamlets earlier” or “Crime figures in these parts have increasing in recent years”. 

The data displayed through the Prayer Companion were carefully gathered, filtered, and curated before being displayed on the device. Several times an hour, sentences were pulled from RSS news feeds and social media posts from around the globe to create a data corpus for processing. The backend system would randomly select a headline from each feed and combine it with social media sentences from the 'I feel fine' API at a ratio of 4:1. The curated batch of messages would then be checked for length to catch overly long sentences, and for illegal characters (as the display could only show a standard ASCII character set) before being sent to the device. Once a batch of messages had been displayed, a new batch was processed, randomly chosen from the remaining corpus. The dynamic nature of news and social media content ensured the corpus was constantly being updated. 

In contrast, the Plane Tracker combined a variety of seemingly disparate data sources to create a new experience from familiar content. Transponder signals from passing aircraft were captured using a shortwave radio scanner. These codes were then cross-referenced on a popular plane spotting website to ascertain the aircraft's origin and destination. This information was then used to recreate an accelerated depiction of the journey the aircraft had taken, using Google Earth. Data from this journey was also employed to control a mechanised globe situated next to the Plane Tracker's screen, illustrating the aircraft's position on the globe. 

#### **3.3 Hybrid data** 

We have also engineered devices that amalgamate readymade data with captured contributions. For instance, the Datacatcher features a mode whereby users can answer a multiple-choice questionnaire about their current area, responding to questions such as: “How do you feel here? (scared / unsafe / on guard / comfortable / secure / nannied / smug)” and “What are the politics around here? (friendly fascist / neo-liberal / weekend radical / anarchy rules / not bothered)”. Their answers are geotagged and incorporated into the existing data corpus so that other Datacatcher devices visiting the same area could subsequently access the updated data. 

Conversely, the Babble device blends readymade data, tweets, and messages from participants in a way that intentionally creates a feedback loop of information. This mix generates a polyphony of voices, details, and concepts pertaining to energy demand and usage. Operating like an automated talk radio station, Babble broadcasts live tweets from the National Grid, plays jingles, and presents an hourly news summary. Messages from official sources, sustainability advocacy groups, and Babble users are merged via a Markov Chain algorithm to add to the continuous stream of conversation around energy-related topics. 

#### **4 ISSUES AND CHALLENGES** 

Through our experiences working with readymade data as a design material, we have become more accustomed to practices like the data wrangling, post-production and hybridisation we have described. Readymade data can present other potential difficulties, however, that we currently address on a more case-by-case basis. Here we discuss three further factors that can make readymade data a challenging material to work with. 

#### **4.1 Data gets old** 

Not surprisingly, stable datasets will age if they are not updated, and this can make designs that rely on them seem out of date after a time. For instance, Drift Table relies on an extensive archive of aerial photography captured by GetMapping.com in 1999. This data is stored on the device’s hard drives and has never been updated, and thus is almost 

23 

25 years old at the time of writing. This can lead to strange anomalies – for instance, we moved to a new studio a few years after producing the Drift Table, and were surprised to find, once we had positioned its view over our new location, that it showed an empty building site. For the most part, however, aging does not seem to affect the experience offered by the Drift Table too badly, because most views are of sparsely developed landscapes and unfamiliar townscapes, and it would take quite significant disruptions to render the landscape it shows clearly anachronistic. 

Landscapes may age slowly, but other datasets become out of date much more quickly. When the Prayer Companion was acquired by the Museum of Modern Art for its permanent design collection, for example, we considered using an archival set of data from its time at the monastery for ease of preservation. Given its reliance on current news items, however, it would quickly have become a historical relic rather than a living system. Thus, with the support of the Museum’s exemplary digital curation team, we reimplemented the software locally so it continues scraping RSS feeds (though these too need to be updated periodically). On the other hand, we did archive the “I feel” social media messages on the assumption that the individual experiences they reflected (happiness, loneliness, the desire to party, etc.) were likely unlikely to age. Time will tell whether we were right. 

#### **4.2 Data has a topography** 

As will be clear from our preceding discussion, we often use spatial metaphors when approaching large datasets – we drift over them, map them to each other, etc. In our experience, such metaphors are useful and rarely if ever lead to trouble. It is important to recognize, however, that the ‘space’ of data is not the uniform, featureless space of Cartesian coordinates, but more like the natural landscape, with hills and valleys, relatively featureless planes and areas of intense development. 

The idea that dataspaces have topographies became particularly evident to us as we developed the Photostroller. The effect we wanted to achieve was one of using the control dial to focus on image categories and the slider to broaden the search – together, these would define the photographs that would drift over the device’s display. Our initial attempts to achieve this used a variety of tactics to ‘drift’ semantically between the tags used to retrieve photographs, for instance by using a thesaurus to find tags related to the ones originally chosen. No matter what we tried, however, we could not realise the effect we wanted. Instead, every attempt to drift semantically among related tags seemed to end up with pictures of flowers or smiling children. We began to realise that these categories were like ‘strange attractors’ in tag space, with many more paths leading to them than away. In the end, we had to hand-code searches to achieve the kind of drifting we wanted. 

Often, it seems that the topology of dataspaces is precisely what our designs are intended to explore. For instance, in bringing together multiple, geographically organised datasets, the Datacatcher implicitly allows correlations amongst them to emerge – for instance, whether unemployment is related to crime, or the number of nearby pubs to life satisfaction. Similarly, Local Barometer assumes that the content of classified advertisements will correlate meaningfully with the location of neighbourhoods. Nonetheless, it is important to recognise that, at least for some datasets, data may be spread unevenly or be politically biased [11,13,41], and that this can work against the intentions of systems that use them. 

#### **4.3 Data is messy – and sometimes dirty** 

As our discussion of post-processing suggests, data is often messy in the sense that does not always fit the requirements of a system – for instance, the source material might be too long, or contain illegal characters, or require translation into natural language. Beyond its technical characteristics, however, data may also be messy in a cultural sense. 

One of the sources of data used by the Datacatcher, for instance, were messages that either mentioned or originated from locations near the device’s current location. While many of these provided useful clues about areas, a substantial subset involved insulting messages about specific people tagged with places (e.g., “Janelle is a bitch! @lewisham”) that 

24 

seemed clearly irrelevant and distracting for our purposes. Interestingly, and fortunately, we found that we could get rid of most items in this genre simply by filtering out messages that contained swear words. 

In contrast, we faced a dilemma when considering the use of social media content for the Prayer Companion: while some of the things people post did not seem appropriate for a group of cloistered nuns, we were not sure we should filter or censor content since the system was meant to give a relatively unbiased view of the current zeitgeist. In the end, we opted to present the content as found, which meant that the nuns were sometimes confronted with messages we found uncomfortable (“I feel sexy, oh so sexy…”). Fortunately, the nuns tended to laugh these off or simply averted their eyes, rightly pointing out to us that they had not always been nuns – which taught us more about the nuns than if we had secondguessed what they were willing to see. 

#### **4.4 Data in a post-truth era** 

A final issue for using data as a design material has to do with the varied orientations people take to it, particularly in the current era of ‘alternative facts’ and ‘fake news’. 

We have often designed systems that encourage people to find their own interpretations of data [51] (see also [3]), much as academics are trained to scrutinise the provenance of data rather than accepting it at face value. For instance, the Home Health Horoscope uses automatically generated horoscopes to evoke a sense of ‘playing with’ the prospect that wellbeing can be measured using sensors, without completely believing or disbelieving the possibility. Similarly, treating classified advertisements as clues about the sociocultural characteristics of areas surrounding the home, as the Local Barometer does, is meant to promote speculation rather than belief. In general, we often seek to use data while undermining its authority in order to empower users to find their own interpretations. 

Sometimes, however, the growing tendency for people to distrust data can undercut the intended effects of systems that seek to deliver uncomfortable truths. For instance, participants’ attitudes towards the messages presented by the Datacatchers varied widely. In part, we intended these to highlight issues such as living conditions in different areas, and income inequality in general. We found, however, that many people assimilated the information presented by the devices into their existing beliefs or interpreted it in terms of personal gain (“estate agents could use these to help people figure out which areas to avoid”). Even more striking was the tendency to discount the data as old or inaccurate when it disagreed with their beliefs, in ways verged on accusing Datacatchers of using fake news. Only occasionally were people struck by the inequalities exposed by the data in the way we hoped. 

Examples like these illustrate a conundrum of dealing with data. On the one hand, it seems valuable for people to retain authority over their own interpretations rather than capitulating to ‘objective’ data. On the other, it is increasingly apparent how dangerous abandoning the notion of facts can be. Moreover, this is an issue that is deeply entangled with politics, since people will disagree about what should be treated as open to interpretation or unassailable. Like challenges regarding data aging, topography, and messiness, we do not have a settled approach to this issue. In practice, we try to address it with moral responsibility, leaving issues open to interpretation when possible, but not when this might lead to misguided conclusions. For instance, the Plane Tracker revealed ambivalence between the allure of travel and the effects on climate change that it implies. We believe exposing this tension is valuable, whereas we would have been concerned had the Plane Tracker raised doubts about the existence of climate change itself. More generally, we see the tensions between interpretation and facts as a challenge for future research, and one that we will address in our design practice.

#### **5 CONCLUSION** 

Data is a rich, nuanced and dynamic material for design that, when framed carefully, can be used to create systems for a wide variety of settings, topics and experiences. In this paper we have drawn on over twenty years of experience designing with data, taking an emergent, annotated portfolio approach to draw out commonalities from ten distinct designs developed by our studio in a post hoc, empirical fashion. This has allowed us to recognise strategies for designing with data, to articulate some of the activities that crafting with data entails, and to discuss issues and challenges for working with data from a hands-on point of view. 

We believe that our bottom-up, emergent approach [29] to understanding what it means to design with data may complement more conceptually-driven and abstract approaches well. As we have described, we did not produce the work here with the aim of investigating how to design with data. Indeed, we seldom considered data as anything but another material to manipulate, and like physical matter the outcome of working with data was far from certain at the beginning of the design process. Instead, the designs we describe here were all focused on fitting with, exposing and extending various settings and issues, from daydreaming in the home to investigating inequality in different parts of the UK. The choice to use data in our designs, the strategies we invented, the methods we developed, the successes we found and the challenges we face – all these came about through pragmatic decisions we made as we worked to craft systems that are engaging both conceptually and in use. 

Given their provenance, our observations in this paper have the nature of empirical findings from the field. As such, they can both serve as a resource, and perhaps corrective, to more theoretical explorations. If nothing else, our designs provide existence proofs that data can be used successfully to produce approachable and compelling experiences, that they can raise issues and resource possible answers without imposing perspectives, and that they can go far beyond the domains addressed by commercial systems to complicate, question and enrich our experiences. They also suggest that there is enormous scope for further development of designs that use data – still, we hope to have demonstrated a start in the right direction.

In contrast to our earlier publications, this paper foregrounds our use of data in designs, delving into the approaches explored, techniques developed, and challenges encountered. We adopt an annotated portfolios approach—which builds on our previous work—for an in-depth exploration. 

We anticipate that this TOCHI submission will provide substantial additional value due to the emerging importance of 'designing with data'. The paper will afford readers a thorough, empirically anchored, and practice-oriented comprehension of the role and usage of data in how we create our research products. Consequently, this paper, while building upon our previous work, takes a unique direction and constitutes an original contribution distinct from our most closely related earlier publications.
