# **Design thinking: Standing on the shoulders of … graphic design!** 

Robert G. Harland School of Design and Creative Arts, Loughborough University <u>r.g.harland@lboro.ac.uk https://orcid.org/0000-0002-8334-3417</u> 

Yaron Meron School of Architecture, Design, and Planning, University of Sydney <u>yaron.meron@sydney.edu.au https://orcid.org/0000-0002-7523-8748</u> 

# **Abstract** 

Prominent design discourse or advocacy in the domain of “design thinking” rarely depicts graphic design consistently, or with sufficient rigour and depth of understanding about the field’s role in the development of design studies. Nor do most advocates for graphic design proffer it to be little more than a vernacular activity when competing for academic attention, despite its widespread academic presence, industry prominence, and everyday practice. The arguments presented in this paper offer a timely critical perspective on a frequently unchallenged prevailing discourse that has echoed consistent assumptions over several decades. The authors call for more integrity in respect for those who design in different communication contexts, more precision in discussing the way graphic design has evolved and been portrayed, and more rigour in the thoroughness and care that research into graphic design and its associated fields now demands as the subject is taught in Universities around the world. The outcomes will be of particular interest to researchers who draw on and re-present Buchanan’s four orders of design concept and offer an alternative perspective to those who suggest graphic design relies overly on intuition when deliberating on design thinking. 

**Keywords** : Design thinking, four orders of design, graphic design, narrative problems

# **Introduction** 

In discussions about design thinking, definitions and depictions of graphic design escape critical scrutiny. Leading design theorists and commentators on the topic reside at opposite ends of the spectrum in their acknowledgement of graphic design’s relative importance. On the one hand, graphic design is of “first order” significance in the revolution in design thinking said to be happening at the turn of the millennium,<sup>1</sup> substantiating the earlier role of commercial art in the materialisation of design.<sup>2</sup> Design thinking as a phrase was then poised to escalate over the first two decades of the twentyfirst century.<sup>3</sup> On the other hand, professional graphic design is dismissed for its intuitive rather than critical stance on design thinking, compared to the “immense growth” in publications on design thinking since 2010.<sup>4</sup> These contradictions appear to suggest that, (1) graphic design provided one of the foundation stones upon which design and design thinking were built, or (2) the revolution has been more recent and graphic design has played no part. In either scenario, the tools and techniques associated with graphic design thinking<sup>5</sup> are overlooked. This article provides a critical response to these fragmentary and nebulous portrayals of graphic design in the domain of design thinking. In doing so, we aspire to advance the cause of design thinking, graphic design thinking, and graphic design research by challenging ongoing assertions in the discourses that impact the historical contribution of the field of graphic design in the wider context of design studies. 

The argument is presented from a United Kingdom (UK) perspective as a critical response to inaccurate portrayals of graphic design at the turn of the millennium, and before. This viewpoint contradicts portrayals of graphic design, often emerging from

# **The first order of design: Graphic design** 

The idea for what became known as the four orders of design was first conceptualised in Richard Buchanan’s essay _Wicked Problems in Design Thinking_<sup>42</sup> as “areas” under the moniker of The Doctrine of Placements. The four areas contextualised and, literally, attempted to bring some order and clarity to what Buchanan believed to be the “disciplines of design in the contemporary world”.<sup>43</sup> The idea was further evolved and expanded in a 1999 conference presentation in the United Kingdom that would provide a divergent view on “a revolution in design thinking.”<sup>44</sup> Hence, it is a concept that has been developed and refined over time both by Buchanan and others. In this article, we engage with the concept as a device to help understand how graphic design has come to be contextualised within the wider discourse of design practice.

Determined as four domains of design thinking, area one was aligned to the design “discipline” of graphic design, which was said to include typography and advertising, book and magazine production, and scientific illustration. In an “expanded” domain that embraced communications theory and semiotics, and through the addition of photography, film, television, and computer display, it was implied this had become “communications design”. This signalled a move away from early graphic design, transformed from its “bookish past” and repositioned as an interpreter of messages. Fast forward a decade or so and these “areas” were renamed as “orders” in 2001.

# **_The depictions of graphic design as the first of four orders of design_** 

Despite Buchanan’s reframing of graphic design as communications design in 1992, graphic design was still named in 2001 by Buchanan as the first of four orders in design theory as it had evolved during the early and middle phases of the twentieth century. Each order of design was conceived as a place for “rethinking and reconceiving the nature of design” in the spirit of discovery, rather than concrete categories where meaning is fixed. In keeping with this spatial analogy, the four orders were depicted in tabular form, showing a step change in the alignment between to the design areas and their respective concerns. By default, the numerical ordering and visual depiction implied a temporal factor in the relationship between the first and second order, compared to the third and fourth order concerns. This is supported by the suggestion that “… designers have turned towards action and environment from symbols and things’ and a concern for visual symbols through the communication of information in words and images.”<sup>45</sup> 

The depiction of graphic design as a first design order and sometimes taught as if it were a decorative subset of, or supporting actor, in relation to other forms of design, would not be so alarming had the same arguments for establishing new learning in the context of design knowledge not placed a high priority on the importance of the role of definition in design. As stated “… definitions are critical for advancing inquiry, and we must face that responsibility from time to time and introduce new ones.”<sup>46</sup> In this article that responsibility is embraced on the basis that not only is a limited use of language restrictive to the understanding of graphic design,<sup>47</sup> but so too are the attempts to recast the field with a new name.

**_The revolution in UK graphic design as it had evolved in post-war decades_** 

Buchanan’s so-called “revolution” in design thinking as it was presented at the London Design Council in 1999 would not have been recognisable to design practitioners in the audience. It is also debatable they would have understood his arguments that graphic design had morphed into communication design. Furthermore, despite claims by Potter, noted earlier, communication design would be a relatively unrecognisable term in the UK professional design sector. However, our argument is not just about terminology. The positionality of graphic design as an academic discourse in its own right has appeared problematic for as long as attempts to elevate design’s prominence alongside art and science have been made. This is perhaps partially because of the disciplinary background of many of the founders of twentieth century design research, with few emerging from graphic design. Graphic design has long been an adaptive practice, in gestation under a number of names and references, before eventually settling on its dominant industry title. But discussions around nomenclature are a symptom rather than the problem. As previous research suggests, while graphic designers may not love the term, they often dislike the alternatives just as much – if not more.<sup>84</sup> The reasons for this are often opaque, but notions around value and design capital seem to emerge regularly.

Critically engaging with design research positioning models, such as the four orders, suggests an almost institutional historical positioning of graphic design as a purely aesthetic practice – an unsophisticated supporting discipline, in relation to interaction and environmental design. Thus, it is not unreasonable to conclude that this has contributed to graphic design’s relative lack of influence within academic research. As such, our concerns with attempts to rename the discipline are not aesthetic or emotive. Instead, we 

have argued that graphic design has emerged as the contemporarily predominant term as the result of an evolving and generative historical disciplinary process. Attempts at renaming the practice, without rigorous discursive research and positioning, are likely symptomatic reactions in response to deeper issues from within broader design hegemonies. 

As design theorists continue to speculate on the usefulness of Buchanan’s four orders approach, and re-orientate it from a spatial approach to a more temporal interpretation, the foundation of design thinking seemingly has at least one foot standing on the shoulders of graphic design practice and theory. In this article, we have contemplated on why this is the case.
