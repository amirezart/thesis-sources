## **ABSTRACT** 

In this paper, we investigate _Research through Design_ at a micro-level, by addressing the dynamic interplay of research and design as they unfold throughout a design process. As our principal case, we consider the design of a three-dimensional projection installation, a process that unfolded over a one-year period. We analyse material collected from 18 key events during the process, in order to identify the ongoing dynamics _._ Based on the analysis, we establish how the interplay evolves in a complex structure, where the design and research interests continuously _couple_ , _interweave,_ and _decouple_ . Last, the paper discusses what facilitates the three different kinds of dynamics.

## **INTRODUCTION** 

Over the past twenty years, _Research through Design_ has emerged as a key means of understanding research practice in the field of interaction design. Previous research has predominantly focused on clarifying the fundamental conceptual distinctions of _Research through Design_ . To advance the research field, we claim that it is productive to investigate _Research through Design_ at a micro-level, by addressing the dynamics of research and design as unfolding throughout a design process. 

In 1993 Christopher Frayling wrote the article ‘Research in Art and Design’ [12]. The article and its triad of research approaches – _research into art and design_ , _research for art and design_ , and _research through art and design_ – have become an influential point of departure for many interaction design researchers, when explaining, discussing,

Frayling does not offer a clear and precise definition of what constitutes _research through art and design_ , but limits himself to offering three main subcategories: material research, development work, and action research, with the common characteristic of involving a practical or experimental approach to generating knowledge, which is communicated to others. 

Based on Frayling’s concept of _Research through Design_ , Zimmerman, Forlizzi, and Evenson [23] have developed a model of _Research through Design_ in HCI research. The model involves a number of key researchers within the HCI research community (interaction designers, anthropologists, behavioural scientists, etc.), as well as the pathways and deliverables among them. At the core of the model are research artefacts (models, prototypes, products, and documentation of design processes), which embody knowledge, and may influence both research and practice communities [23]. In their paper, Zimmerman and his coresearchers also offer three lenses through which design research contributions may be evaluated, and demonstrate how these lenses function in three brief historical cases, but they do not apply their own model of _Research through Design_ to any specific design case or design process. 

In their 2006 paper, _Exemplary Design Research_ [3] Binder and Redström introduced the terms _question_ , _programme_ , and _experiment_ to address Exemplary Design Research; ‘exemplary’ in that it facilitates critical dissemination primarily through examples of what could be done, and how. ‘Programme’ defines the area of exploration, and identifies the overall goal of an activity, whereas ‘experiment’ is the concept that encompasses the concrete activities. ‘Question’ refers to the overarching research question that guides a research project, and it is through a combination of ‘programme’ and ‘experiments’ that the research question is addressed, and, most importantly, knowledge is attained. 

In 2007, Binder and Brandt [4] developed a model of the relation between ‘question’, ‘programme’, and ‘experiment’. Moreover, they elaborated on the interrelations among the three elements in design practice, and the three elements in design research, but they did not address the relation between design activities and research activities as parts of academic practice.

In his 2009 dissertation, Peter Dalsgaard [7] elaborates further on the Binder and Brandt model. Dalsgaard splits the model into two parts – a research programme guided by an overarching question, and a design programme guided by an overarching contract with the stakeholders in the design project. In a subsequent paper, Dalsgaard [8] presents the model and applies it to a brief analysis of a design case in which interaction design researchers and architects collaborated in an architectural competition for the design of a building with a media façade. As part of the analysis, some of the tensions and conflicts relating to the programme of the interaction design researchers, and the contract of the architects are identified. 

Like Dalsgaard our interest in _Research through Design_ lies in aspects related to the design _process_ rather than the designed product. We understand _Research through Design_ as an activity where researchers are actively engaged in the design process and knowledge is communicated among a research community through academic journals, conferences, workshops, and so on. 

At our research laboratory we have, over the past year, developed and tested a tool, the Process Reflection Tool (PRT) [10], for documenting and reflecting on design processes. The tool continuously allows project participants to document actions and reflections in relation to a particular design project. We believe the tool meets a need to focus more consciously on documenting the design process, as pointed out by Zimmerman and his co-authors [24]. The tool will be presented in greater detail later. 

One of the design processes documented by the PRT concerns the design project for the installation of _The Journey of Holger the Dane._ In this paper, we will analyse the collected material, to identify the ongoing dynamics of _Research through Design_ . The starting point for this analysis is _Research through Design_ ’s embedded nature, which involves the necessity of handling the challenge of wanting to inform a research question, while also having to care about the installation that, from a design perspective, will be the end product of the process. In the analysis, we examine how the dynamic interplay between research and design interests evolves, and what constitutes them. 

This paper is structured as follows: This introduction has stated how our analysis adds a micro-perspective to the ongoing conceptual distinctions of _Research through Design_ . In the next section, we present the case of _The Journey of Holger the Dane_ , before turning to the microanalysis of the dynamic interplay unfolding between research and design interests. The paper finally discusses what facilitates the three different dynamics: _coupling_ , _interweaving_ , and _decoupling_ . 

## **METHOD** 

The findings presented in this paper are the results of an analysis of a particular design process for the installation _The Journey of Holger the Dane_ . One author participated in all parts of the design process, and the other author followed the design process from the sidelines. Two students, with backgrounds in information studies and ethnography, assisted with the documentation of the process, by keeping minutes and reporting on each event and meeting in the process, and by video recording complex events, where minutes would have been insufficient. 

The documentation of the process was accomplished via the Process Reflection Tool, or PRT, which has been developed by our research laboratory as a general tool for documenting design processes for research purposes. The software tool is structured in a blog-like format as a list of events, which in turn may be described in greater detail in terms of sub-events. It is possible for the project participants to add their reflections to each event and subevent. For example, an event may be a planning meeting, a field study, a workshop, or a prototype test. In addition to events, the tool supports ‘brief annotations’ documenting telephone conversations, short e-mail exchanges, and the like. Video and still images may be uploaded and associated with each event, sub-event, and note, as additional material supporting the documentation of the process. Moreover, and most importantly, the PRT supports the association of reflection on and analysis of the materials with each of the elements, enabling analysis across the project, with regard to specific aspects of the process, such as in the current instance, _Research through Design_ . The addition of reflections and analysis may be ongoing, or occur once the design process is complete. 

The philosophy of the PRT tool is rooted in Donald Schön’s theory [20] of the reflective practitioner, and built on previously developed strategies and tools for mapping and reflecting on design processes, including Mathiassen’s and Lanzara’s [18] maps for addressing complex situations in systems development, and Dalsgaard [6] et al.’s research into the use of maps for exploring design materials and sources of inspiration in design processes. 

The Holger the Dane project’s process has been documented as 18 events, most of which, in turn, have been described in greater detail by between two and ten subevents. The main part of the design process evolved during a one-year period.

As our principal case, we will consider the design of a three-dimensional projection installation,<sup>1</sup> _The Journey of Holger the Dane_ , projected on a white concrete statue of the legendary Viking warrior, Holger the Dane [15]. The statue is located in a casemate in Denmark’s Kronborg Castle, a nearly 400-year-old, World Heritage listed renaissance castle.

Three-dimensional projection on physical objects is a particular kind of spatially Augmented Reality (AR), which augments a physical object by projecting digital content directly onto it. The basic process behind 3D projection installations starts with a physical object, and the creation of a 3D model of the object. The next step is to modify the 3D model, for instance, by changing one of the surfaces, and to subsequently project the modified 3D model onto the physical object – thereby augmenting the physical object. 

## **The installation** 

In the case of _The Journey of Holger the Dane_ , we have used 3D projection to add a digital layer directly onto the statue, bringing elements of the legends told about Holger the Dane to life for the audience. Great Danish romantic writers such as Grundtvig, Ingemann, and H.C. Andersen have made the general Danish public aware that Holger will awaken and defend the country, when an enemy from without threatens the kingdom of Denmark. However, the legend of Holger’s life is not as well known. Therefore, the digital layer, supported by an audio component, presents different elements of the legend of Holger’s life. 

As part of the installation, we meet the six fairies who endowed Holger the Dane in his cradle with gifts such as immortality, strength, courage, and attractiveness to women. Several of the important and dramatic events in Holger’s life, such as his imprisonment by Carl the Great, and his defeat of the giant Burmand, are staged. Finally, the old warrior finds peace, and falls asleep. For a detailed presentation of the installation, see [15].

## **The design process** 

The design process spanned the period from March to October 2010, and the installation was on display from October 2010 to February 2011. To provide an overview of the rather complex design process, we have organized the process in a timeline consisting of seven main stages: _Point of departure_ , _Framing_ , _Preliminary studies_ , _Workshop_ , _Design decisions_ , _Production_ , and _Display._ 



**Figure 5: Timeline of the main stages of the design process.** 

Participants in the design process were three PhD fellows from Information Studies at Aarhus University, a project manager, an engineer, and three programmers from CAVI. From Kronborg Castle, the palace steward, their publicity 

agent, the exhibition curator, a guide, and the events manager were involved. Moreover, as mentioned in the ‘Method’ section, two project assistants documented the process as minutes and video recordings. 

## _Point of departure_ 

Before the design process gathered speed, a longer period of negotiation with Kronborg took place – in the timeline above, we call this stage of the design process the ‘Point of departure’. As an initial step, CAVI’s project manager approached the palace steward and presented the idea, referencing one of CAVI’s previous projection projects in the field of cultural heritage communication. The palace steward’s immediate reaction was somewhat reluctant, because the statue is symbolically loaded.<sup>2</sup> The project manager explained that, in line with the design philosophy of our laboratory, castle representatives would directly participate in the design process, which then raised the concern about the extent of the resources the castle might be expected to put into the project. After a several-monthlong process involving an on and off dialogue between the palace steward and the CAVI project manager, it was finally agreed to start a joint project. 

## _Framing and preliminary studies_ 

After having reached a cooperative arrangement with Kronborg, and thereby having the point of departure for the _design interest_ settled, researchers from our Information Studies department were invited to participate in the project. When determining whom to invite, the project manager contacted the centre’s director, who coordinates the different research and PhD projects. Three PhD fellows responded to the invitation, and their research questions framed the project’s research focus, along with the overall focus on three-dimensional projection. The focuses of the three PhD projects were, briefly: affective engagement, site as design resource, and engaging experiences. 

During the first meeting between the project manager and the PhD fellows involved, they discussed how to create a joint point of departure for the process. It was agreed that a preliminary study should be carried out at Kronborg, to determine what characterized the location, and how the visitors behaved in front of the statue. The purpose of the preliminary studies was to inform the planning of a workshop. 

## _Workshop and design decisions_

aim of the activities was to keep the workshop fast-paced, in order to facilitate constant reframing of the situation and exploration of the relationship between different experiential qualities and means during the workshop. 

The purpose of the Site Tour, which was a guided tour of the castle’s casemates, was to ensure that all participants had a vivid recollection of the story of Holger the Dane, as well as a sense of the place for which they were designing. 

The purpose of the next phase, the _Inspiration Brainstorm_ , was to address different experiential qualities of the design setting, by building relationships between experiential means, in order to strengthen the emotional and sensory threads of experience. Inspired by _Inspiration Card Workshops_ [14], each group was provided with three different sets of ideation cards: _emotion cards_ , _sense cards_ , and _obstruction cards._ Each group had to draw three cards; an _emotion_ , a _sense_ , and an _obstruction_ card, and was then given five minutes to brainstorm and combine the cards into design ideas and elements for an unfolding experience of the Holger the Dane statue. The ideation procedure was repeated seven times, resulting in a range of preliminary design concepts. 

After the Inspiration Brainstorming, the groups presented their concepts to one another. To conclude this phase of the workshop, the groups had to choose the most interesting findings from the Inspiration Brainstorms. These perspectives, ideas, and concepts were then composed into spatio-temporal scenarios in the _Sequencing_ section. The goal of the _Presentation_ was to share ideas among participants, and establish a reflective stance on the design actions thus far. 

The S _equencing_ section of the workshop was a dramaturgic session involving the staging of the statue of Holger the Dane. For this section, the groups where provided with a cardboard box resembling the casemate in which the statue is situated. Additionally, a small souvenir model of Holger the Dane was provided, along with a number of LEGO figures that could function as the visitors. 

In the final and conclusive phase of the workshop, the groups had to shoot small video scenarios of their design concepts unfolding in time and space. The videos were shot in the cardboard settings, with LEGO figures and other props, to which were added live commentaries and voiced sound effects. The aim was to combine and document the ideas from the previous phases of the workshop. 

Following the workshop, the project group used the subsequent period to make a number of design decisions, by summing up the workshop material and looking at interaction possibilities. A number of scenarios for the future experience of the Holger the Dane statue were written up, and the sequencing for the installation was planned. 

## _Production_ 

At this point, the 3D designers at our laboratory became involved, and to a large extent took over the process. For the production, the statue was scanned and a 3D print, at 1:4 scale, was made, to test the projections as they evolved. On a weekly basis, the researchers were invited to view the ongoing work with the projections, and to comment on them. At this point, the composer of the audio components worked closely with the project manager, to make sure that the audio component would fit the visuals. 

## _On display, and evaluation_ 

The installation was on display at Kronborg Castle from October 2010 to March 2011. During that period, two visitor surveys were carried out through observation and interviews focusing on the visitors’ experience and understanding of the installation. 

## **A DYNAMIC PERSPECTIVE ON THE PROCESS** 

We now present the analytical section of this paper. By analysing the empirical material via the process reflection tool, PRT, the complexity of the interplay between the research interests and the design interests was revealed. It also became clear that the design process could be divided into four main parts. The divisions stem from the forms of interplay identified, and are in chronological order: 

- Coupling the point of departure for the design and research interests. 

- Interweaving design and research interests with the Composition Workshop as platform 

- Focusing on design interests during production 

- Inquiring into the research interests, evaluating the design 

## **Coupling design and research interests** 

The first part of the analysis explores the initial coupling of the research and the design interests. This coupling is the part of the process where the design and the research interests establish the common point of departure for the main portion of the design process.

Also, the idea of using Holger the Dane as a subject brought a number of domainspecific constraints to the project, with respect to site and ethical codes. A third set of factors present from the outset of the project included the deadline, the budget, and technical limitations. These factors affected both the design and research interests. For example, one of the PhD fellows would have liked to have the installation running in a matrix pattern, reacting to factors such as the number of visitors present in the room. However, the deadline and the technical limitations precluded this idea. The fact that the idea was precluded might lead this PhD fellow to establish a new research project, in which that idea might be tested. In this way, the interplay extends beyond this one project. 

During the _Framing_ period, the PhD fellows and the project manager met to clarify the PhD fellows’ interests in the project, and whether these interests could be unfolded in the framework of the project, and whether that coupling would bring the design project nearer to a final state that would meet the expectations of the stakeholders involved. 

As an example, one of the PhD fellows saw the project as an interesting framework for inquiring into and evolving methods for mapping site-specific elements, both in terms of the physical setting, and in terms of the visitors’ actions in front of the statue, and reactions to it. This perspective was also important to the _design interests_ , because the installation was to unfold around a symbolically loaded statue in a World Heritage Site, which therefore forced the project group to carefully consider the site-specific elements. 

Further on in the process, the interest in the representation of site-specific elements led to the _Site Tour_ , and the workshop’s use of the two-dimensional floor plans and the cardboard boxes representing the casemates. 

Further on again, during the evaluation and writing of papers, the interest in representation of site-specific elements led to an inquiry into how different representations of the statue’s location in the casemate affected the participants’ manner of working with the site during the workshop.

The second part of the analysis focuses on how the interplay between research and design interests interweaves them, and how they mutually influence each other in the planning, winding-up, and results evaluation of the Composition Workshop.

In the case of _The Journey of Holger the Dane_ , the sections of the process in which the main materials for the design were created are the _Workshop_ and _Decision making_ periods. The dynamic interplay identified is illustrated by the boxes, and will be elaborated below. 

The Composition Workshop was planned for three purposes: first, to provide empirical material for the research interests involved, second, to engage the Kronborg staff in the design, and third to move the process forward, by creating initial conceptualizations of the future experience of the Holger the Dane statue. With these three purposes clarified, the workshop could be organized in detail. 

To a large extent, the research interests determined the planning and organization. First, the planning reflected CAVI’s general research interest in 3D projections. By presenting a previous projection installation, _The Mejlby Stone installation_ [1], also in the area of cultural heritage communication, the workshop was steered in the direction of turning the Holger the Dane statue into a 3D projection installation. At the same time, the framing of the project as a 3D projection case also had an impact on how the research interests of the three PhD fellows unfolded. For instance, further presentation and explanation of technology was omitted from the workshop, leaving room to focus on the three research perspectives: affective engagement, engaging experiences, and site as design resource. These perspectives were the second element framing the planning and organization of the workshop. This is most clearly seen in the choice and development of design methods for the Composition Workshop. 

The methods for designing the workshop were chosen and developed in two iterations. The first iteration included a meeting with the PhD fellows, where their methods and the anticipated outcomes were discussed, to ensure that everyone’s particular research interests were addressed. Methods were selected primarily on the basis of their providing research insight into emotions, perception, and site-specific elements, by including materials that enabled

the participants to brainstorm about which feeling and emotions each associated with Holger the Dane, and the statue’s location. In a subsequent iteration, the focus was on how the outcome of the workshop would generate a catalogue of ideas to advance the design process. During the workshop, the focus was primarily on the _design interests_ . When looking though the videos of the two groups working on the staging of the statue, this focus clearly stands out. At the same time, it is striking how the PhD fellows, through their questions, turn the discussion towards research-relevant subjects. E.g.: ‘If we think of gaming as something we do with our bodies, in the area in front of the statue, how would we interact with the installation?’ 

Following the workshop, the project group had a considerable quantity of material, consisting of posters, filmed scenarios, and video documentation of the five-hour event. These materials later served as both design and research material. As an example, all the concrete design ideas from the posters and scenarios were collected in a catalogue, which was used as inspiration for the subsequent stages of the design process, during which the installation sequence took place. At the same time, the posters were interesting research material, when considering representations of site as design resource, because the pre-printed floor plan was used differently by the two groups. One group sketched in their ideas as part of the room, and in relation to the statue, while the other group ignored the floor plan, and covered it with notes and stickers. 

The same pattern applies to the cardboard boxes and the LEGO figures. As design material, they created a setting for discussing future scenarios with regard to both location and physical experience. At the same time, the video of these discussions comprises important research material, when looking at the influence of physical, spatial representation.

Metaphorically speaking, it may be said that the interwoven research and design interests were untangled into research results and concrete design ideas – _Research through Design_ . 

## **Focusing on design interests** 

This section of the analysis examines the production stage of the process, which is characterized by a focus on the design interests, in terms of completing the installation. As previously the boxes illustrate the interplay.

The conspicuous focus on the design interests is best explained by the way the project group worked during the production period. The production period was characterized by an iterative structure. The point of departure was the storyline, which the project manager wrote, based on the information and insights from the preceding events in the design process. Through twelve iterations, the elements in the storyline were discussed, adjusted, and produced. First, the technical staff provided a number of suggestions for the visuals, then the project group decided which one fitted the overall idea, then the technical staff produced a more complete version, which was again discussed by the project group. The discussions primarily concerned how the projections functioned with the statue, and whether the full potential of the statue’s physical appearance was exploited. The group also discussed how the audio component supported the atmosphere that they wanted the various elements of the installation to create.

Once the installation was put on display, the focus on the design interests ended. The sound level was adjusted once during the period, but apart from that, the design of the installation was not changed. 

The visitor surveys reflected the research interests. The first survey was conducted by two of the PhD fellows observing the visitors’ reactions to the installation, and interviewing them about the experience. Even though the surveys were conducted to inform the research of the three PhD fellows, the survey also provided valuable information about the design. ## **KINDS OF DYNAMICS** 

The foregoing analysis suggests that the interplay between research and design interests evolves through an ongoing, dynamic process, in this case, starting with the coupling of separate design and research interests, and proceeding to their being thoroughly interwoven then subsequently untangled, and moreover, going from design and research interests being equal focuses, to one or the other being the conspicuous focus. 



## **Figure 12: Overview of the described dynamics.** 

The above analysis, which is based on a single case, must be seen as a first step towards a better understanding of the underlying dynamics of the interplay between research and design interests underlying a design process, and thereby a more thorough understanding of _Research through Design_ . Through the analysis, we identified three types of dynamics: 

- Coupling 

- Interweaving 

- Decoupling 

_Coupling_ is a dynamic that unites design and research interests, and thereby establishes a framework for, as well as a set of constraints on the further steps in the process. 

_Interweaving_ is a dynamic wherein one activity or material informs both design and research interests. 

_Decoupling_ is a dynamic that modifies the focus, by turning either design or research interests into the salient focus of the process. 

The three different dynamics unfold in unique ways during a given design process. Next, using the case of _The Journey of Holger the Dane_ , we will look further into what causes them to occur and evolve. From the above analysis, it may be seen that specific design artefacts (a historical statue, design materials, design experiments, and the projection installation), contributed to the dynamic change in the

interplay between research and design interests. This is in line with Dalsgaard’s [8] findings, when he briefly indicates design artefacts’ capacity to enable dynamics: 

_Although the general structure of the process was planned, it was non-linear in the sense that emerging realisations and findings derived from the exploration of design artefacts would influence the design activities and vice versa_ . 

To better understand the dynamic power of the design artefacts, we will consider them as _boundary objects._ The term _boundary object_ was originally coined by Star and Griesemer [21] to describe an entity that may be used for varying purposes and ascribed different meanings, while at the same time being rigid enough conceptually to form a shared point of reference and a platform for negotiation among �����������������������������. Olav Bertelsen [2] has discussed how design artefacts may be understood as boundary objects, in the sense that, in many cases, design artefacts function as mediators in design processes, by being used differently by different groups with different roles, motivations, and professional backgrounds. In the case of _Research through Design_ , understanding how artefacts mediate between design and research is particularly interesting. 

## _The statue facilitates coupling of design and research interests_ 

The Holger the Dane statue, and the many myths related to it, may be understood as serving as boundary objects between research and design. From the analysis of the process, it is evident that the statue is the point of departure for the phase we call _coupling design and research interests,_ negotiations between the project manager and Kronborg, and the PhD fellows’ reasons for engaging with the project. From a research perspective, focus is on the evocative aspects and emotional qualities associated with the statue, but from a 3D projection research perspective, its complex geometry is also crucial. From a design perspective, the factual, historical elements are the main focus, but also important are values associated with the statue. By serving as a shared point of reference, the statue and its context enable _Coupling_ . 

_Workshop materials facititates interweaving of design and research interests_ 

The cardboard box and the LEGO figures used for the Composition Workshop are also boundary objects. From the analysis of the process, it is evident that when _interweaving design and research interests_ , these materials inform both design and research interests, and thereby interweave the two, which must subsequently be disentangled for design or research purposes. From a research perspective, the materials were used to inquire into how three-dimensional spatial and physical representations facilitate the creation of a future scenario. From a design perspective, the workshop materials supported 

communication about visitors’ patterns of movement during the discussion of design ideas, for example. 

_Design experiments and the installation facilitates decoupling of design and research interests_ 

Both the numerous design experiments conducted during the production period and the final installation functioned as boundary objects that enabled the current focus of the project to start from either design or research interests, and thus, temporarily decoupled them. So, at a given time, they brought either design or research into focus, and hereby, they, at the time, informed either design or research. Thus, the experiments in the lab were carried out solely with a design focus, but were later evaluated against their communicative power, as part of the design process, and later yet, were analysed from the perspective of the research interest in 3D projection. Similarly, while it was on display, the final projection installation was regarded solely as an object of study for researchers interested in engaging experiences, affect, and so forth, but it was also an important part of the castle, and serves as a new form of cultural heritage communication that may inspire future designs in this field. 

As suggested by Carlile [5], everyone has a different goal when using boundary objects. What characterizes the four boundary objects described above is that they function broadly enough to encompass the interests of several different people and groups within the project, and in that they facilitate their dynamics. 

## **CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK** 

This paper uses a case study to investigate _Research through Design_ at a micro-level, and to establish how, by use of various artefacts functioning as boundary objects, the interplay between research and design interests evolves, via a complex dynamic that continuously _couples_ , _interweaves_ , and _decouples_ these two interests. 

We believe that a better understanding of the dynamics affecting the interplay of design and research interests may provide insight that may guide future _Research through Design_ processes, and improve the benefits for both research and design. 

The dynamics and boundary objects discussed here must be considered the results of a preliminary analysis, which may help researchers to improve their understanding of how to document design processes, with the goal of improving the understanding of how design artefacts involved in a design process, such as methods and materials, function to contribute to the dynamics of both research and design. 

We do not claim that the findings may be generalized, but our analysis may be considered a first attempt to inquire into how a well-documented design process may form the basis for understanding the dynamic interplay at work in a _Research through Design_ process.
